Introduction

Recall from last time:

  • The Voronoi tessellation \(V_\Phi\) is the collection of Voronoi cells of points in \(\mathrm{supp}(\Phi)\);

  • If \(\Phi\) is stationary with intensity \(\lambda>0\), then \(V_\Phi\) is a stationary particle process;

  • The typical cell of \(V_\Phi\) is \(Z := V(0, \Phi^0),\) the Voronoi cell of \(0\) under the Palm distribution \(\Phi^0\).

Today: Iversion formula & consequences.

Inversion Formula

Let \(\Phi\) be a stationary point process on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with intensity \(\lambda>0\). The typical cell \(Z\) of \(V_\Phi\) gives the following inversion formula, allowing us to obtain the distribution of \(\Phi\) from the Palm version \(\Phi^0\).

::: theorem **Theorem (Inversion Formula).** For all measurable \(f : \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^d) \to \mathbb{R}_{+}\), \[\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(\Phi)\bigr] = \lambda \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f\bigl(t_x \Phi^0\bigr)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{x \in Z\}}\,dx \right],\] where \(Z := V(0,\Phi^0)\) is the typical cell of \(V_{\Phi}\), and \(t_x \mu(B) := \mu(B+x)\). :::

Some Consequences:

  • Letting \(f \equiv 1\), \(\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z|\bigr] = \frac{1}{\lambda}.\)

  • Define \(Z_{0}(\Phi)\) to be the cell of \(V_{\Phi}\) containing the origin. Let \(f(\Phi) := \frac{1}{|Z_{0}(\Phi)|}.\) Then \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[|Z_{0}(\Phi)|^{-1}\right] = \lambda \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} |Z_{0}(t_x \Phi^0)|^{-1} \mathbf{1}_{\{x \in Z\}}\,dx \right].\]

    If \(x \in Z = V(0,\Phi^0)\), then \(0 \in V\bigl(-x,\,t_x \Phi^0\bigr) \overset{d}{=} V\bigl(0,\Phi^0\bigr)-x,\) implying that \(|Z_{0}(t_x \Phi^0)| = |Z|.\)

    Therefore, \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[|Z_{0}(\Phi)|^{-1}\right] = \lambda\, \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} |Z|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{x \in Z\}}\,dx \right] = \lambda\,\mathbb{E}[1] = \lambda.\] By Jensen’s inequality, \[\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z_{0}(\Phi)|\bigr]^{-1} \;\le\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[|Z_{0}(\Phi)|^{-1}\right] = \lambda = \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z|\bigr]}.\]

    Thus, \[\mathbb{E}[|Z|] \;\le\; \mathbb{E}[|Z_{0}(\Phi)|].\]

    This inequality expresses Feller’s Paradox.

Inversion Formula from a General Result

Define \(\tau : \mathbb{R}^d \times \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^d) \to \mathbb{R}^d\) by letting \(\tau(x,\eta)\) be the point in \(\mathrm{supp}(\eta)\) that is closest to \(x\). Let \(\Phi\) be a stationary point process on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with intensity \(\lambda>0\). Also define \(Y := \tau(0,\Phi)\).

::: theorem **Theorem.** For all measurable \(h : \mathbb{R}^d \times \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^d) \to \mathbb{R}_{+}\), \[\mathbb{E}\bigl[h(Y,\Phi)\bigr] = \lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} h\!\left(-x,\,t_x \Phi^0\right) \mathbf{1}_{\{x \in Z\}}\,dx \right],\] and the inversion formula follows from letting \(h(x,\eta)=f(\eta)\). :::

::: proof *Proof.* Recall refined Campbell’s theorem together with a change of variables:

\[\quad \mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x,\Phi)\,\Phi(dx)\right] = \lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(-x,\,t_x \Phi^0)\,dx\right].\]

Now let \(f(x,\Phi) := h\bigl(x,\Phi\bigr)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau(0,\Phi)=x\}}.\)

The LHS of Eq. [eq:if] equals: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} h(x,\Phi)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau(0,\Phi)=x\}}\, \Phi(dx) \right] &= \mathbb{E}\bigl[h(Y,\Phi)\bigr].\end{aligned}\]

The RHS of Eq. [eq:if] equals: \[\begin{aligned} \quad \lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} h(-x,\,t_x \Phi^0)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau(0,t_x \Phi^0)=-x\}} \,dx \right].\end{aligned}\]

We note that \(\tau\) satisfies the following property: \[\tau(x-y,\,t_y \mu) = \tau(x,\mu)-y.\]

Letting \(y = x\), we have \(\tau(0,t_x\Phi^0)=\tau(x,\Phi^0)-x\). Then \[\tau(0,t_x\Phi^0) = -x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tau(x,\Phi^0)=0.\]

Therefore, \[\begin{aligned} \text{RHS}&= \lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} h(-x,\,t_x \Phi^0)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau(x,\Phi^0)=0\}} \,dx \right] \\ &= \lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} h(-x,\,t_x \Phi^0)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{x \in Z\}} \,dx \right].\end{aligned}\]

\(\square\) :::

A consequence of this more general result:

Let \(h(x,\mu) := g(t_x \mu)\). Then \(h\!\left(-x,\,t_x \Phi^0\right) = g\!\left(t_{-x}t_x \Phi^0\right) = g(\Phi^0),\) and \[\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(t_Y \Phi)\bigr]=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,|Z|\,g(\Phi^0)\right].\]

This shows that the distribution of \(t_Y \Phi\) is absolutely continuous with respect to the Palm distribution.

Further, letting \(g\) in Eq. [eq:g] satisfy \(g(\mu) := f(\mu)\,|Z_{0}(\mu)|^{-1},\) gives \[\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\Phi^0)\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ |Z_{0}(t_Y \Phi)|^{-1}\, f(t_Y \Phi) \right].\]

Since \(Z_{0}(t_Y \Phi) = Z_{0}(\Phi) - Y,\) we obtain the following.

::: proposition **Proposition.** \[\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\Phi^0)\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ |Z_{0}(\Phi)|^{-1}\,f(t_Y \Phi) \right].\] :::

This gives, for \(f(\mu) := |Z_{0}(\mu)|^{\alpha+1},\) the more genral formula \[\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[|Z|^{\alpha+1}\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[|Z_{0}|^{\alpha}\right].\]
